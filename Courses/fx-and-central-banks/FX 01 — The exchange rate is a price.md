Before anything else in the article makes sense: what the number ¥163 actually is, which direction is "weak", and who sets it.

≈7 minutes · Unlocks the article's first paragraph

> He dipped into America's foreign-exchange reserves to help the Bank of Japan (BoJ) buy yen, which had **weakened past ¥163 to the dollar** for the first time since 1986.
>
> The Economist, "When Japan buys yen, it unwinds a dangerous trade", 13 August 2026

## A quote is a ratio, and it has a direction

"¥163 to the dollar" is not a measurement of the yen. It is the *price of one dollar, expressed in yen* — exactly like "£3 for a coffee" is the price of a coffee expressed in pounds. The dollar is the thing being priced; the yen is the ruler.

This is where almost everyone's intuition first slips. If the price of coffee goes from £3 to £5, coffee got more expensive — and the pound bought less of it. Same here. When the quote goes from ¥150 to ¥163, a dollar got more expensive in yen terms, which is the same sentence as: **the yen got weaker**. The number going *up* means the yen going *down*.

The FX market quotes this pair as USD/JPY, and the convention is genuinely inconsistent across pairs — EUR/USD is quoted the other way round, dollars per euro. There is no deep logic; it is market custom. When reading, always ask "which currency is the ruler?" rather than trusting the label.

## Check yourself

The quote moves from ¥150 to ¥163 per dollar. What has happened?

- [ ] The yen has risen and dollars now cost less than they did.
- [ ] The yen has fallen and dollars now cost more than they did.
- [ ] The dollar has fallen and exports now cost more to make.
- [ ] The yen has risen and imports now cost more to buy in.

> [!tip]- Reveal the answer
> The number is yen-per-dollar, so a bigger number means each dollar takes more yen to buy. The dollar is dearer; the yen is weaker. If you hesitated, re-read the ruler sentence above — this single confusion causes more misreadings of FX journalism than anything else.

## Who is actually buying and selling?

The instinctive answer is trade: Japan sells cars to America, gets paid in dollars, sells those dollars for yen to pay its workers. That happens, and it is where the textbook story starts. But it is a rounding error in the modern market.

Global foreign-exchange turnover ran at about **$7.5 trillion per day** in April 2022, according to the BIS Triennial Survey[¹](#s1) — the census of this market. Total world merchandise trade for an entire year is roughly $25 trillion. The FX market turns over the world's annual trade every few days. Trade is not what sets the price.

Turnover is not the same as net demand — a single underlying transaction gets passed between dealers several times and counted each time. The BIS is explicit about this. The point survives anyway: even generously adjusted, financial flows dwarf trade flows.

What sets the price is *portfolio decisions* — investors, banks, pension funds and governments choosing which currency to hold their savings in. And the single biggest input into that choice is the **interest rate you earn in each currency**. If yen deposits pay near zero and dollar deposits pay 4%, money moves toward the dollar, which means selling yen to buy dollars, which pushes the yen down. That mechanism is the engine of everything else in this arc — Lesson 4 gives it its proper name.

> [!abstract] The one-line version
> Over a decade, exchange rates drift with relative prices and productivity. Over a month, they are driven by where the yield is and where people think it is going. The article is entirely about the second timescale.

## Why a country would care

An exchange rate is not a scoreboard of national virtue, though politicians talk about it that way. It is a relative price, and like any relative price it makes some people better off and others worse off. The article states both sides plainly:

> An uncomfortably strong yen hurt Japan's exporters and worsened its deflationary tendencies after its asset-price bubble burst in the 1990s. An excessively weak yen, by contrast, raises the cost of food and fuel, most of which are imported.

Notice that this makes the "right" exchange rate a political question, not a technical one. Japan spent the 1990s and 2000s fighting a yen that was too *strong*, because it was fighting deflation and the strong yen was making deflation worse. In 2026 it is doing the opposite. Nothing about the mechanism changed — the problem being solved changed. Hold onto that: it is the hinge the whole article turns on.

## Retrieval Practice

Without scrolling up: why does the article treat a weak yen as an inflation problem for Japan, rather than as good news for a big exporting nation? Write two sentences.

> [!example]- Model answer
> Japan imports nearly all its food and fuel. Those are priced in dollars, so a weaker yen mechanically raises their yen price, and because everyone must eat and heat, that feeds straight into consumer prices. The export gain is real but concentrated in a few large firms, while the import cost is spread across every household — which is why it dominates politically. Lesson 4 adds the second reason: by 2026 Japan finally has the inflation it spent thirty years wanting, so more of it is no longer a bonus.

## What you can now read

The article's first sentence about the yen "weakening past ¥163 to the dollar for the first time since 1986" should now decode without effort: the yen was cheaper against the dollar than at any point in forty years, imported energy and food were correspondingly expensive for Japanese households, and that is why a government would spend real money to push the number back down.

What it does *not* yet explain is what the government spends. That is [[FX 02 — Reserves, and what it means to intervene|Lesson 2: reserves, and what it means to intervene]].
