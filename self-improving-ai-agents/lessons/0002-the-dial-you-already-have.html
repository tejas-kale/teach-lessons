Lesson 2 of 14 · Self-Improving AI Agents

The dial you already have

Repeated sampling turns a weaker model into a stronger one — predictably, with its own scaling law. Two ways to spend inference compute, and which one to reach for.

≈10 minutes · Lecture 2 · Unlocks: "should I upgrade the model or the harness?"

Here is the result that reorganised the field. Take Llama 3 8B — a small, open, unimpressive model. Ask it the same hard problem 250 times instead of once. Then, using a verifier, pick the correct answer out of the pile. On several math and coding benchmarks that arrangement beats a single call to a much larger frontier model.¹

Nothing about the small model changed. No fine-tuning, no better prompt. The correct answer was already inside its output distribution; the default of sampling once threw it away.

Reframe

Stop thinking of a model as a function that returns an answer. Think of it as a distribution you can draw from, and of your harness as the thing that decides how many draws to take and which to keep. Model quality sets the distribution. You set everything else.

Coverage has a scaling law

Define coverage — usually written pass@k — as the fraction of problems solved by at least one of k samples. The Large Language Monkeys work found that coverage grows with k as an approximate power law, and that the fit holds across model families and across three orders of magnitude of parameter count, from 70M to 70B.¹ On SWE-bench, DeepSeek-V3 with a thousand samples solved more issues than single-shot calls to the frontier models of the day.

Why a power law, when the per-problem maths is exponential? For a single problem with per-sample success probability p, pass@k is 1−(1−p)k — clean exponential saturation. Aggregating across a benchmark only produces a power law if the distribution of p across problems has a long tail of very hard items. It does, empirically. That tail is the reason sampling keeps paying off long after you'd expect it to stop.

Two things follow that matter to you more than the curve itself:

  • You can budget in advance. Fit the curve on a slice of your own workload and you can answer "how many samples to reach 90%?" with a number rather than a shrug. This is the same discipline as a pre-training scaling law, applied to a dial you actually control.
  • The gains come from the tail. Easy problems are solved on the first try; extra samples buy you nothing there. All of the value is concentrated in the hard items, which is precisely where per-sample cost is highest. Sampling uniformly across your workload wastes most of the money.

Parallel or sequential — two ways to spend

Repeated sampling is only one shape. The other is sequential revision: let the model draft, look again, and improve its own answer over several turns. Snell et al. studied both and, importantly, studied when each wins.²

Which to reach for

Snell et al.'s rule of thumb, binned by problem difficulty: easier problems favour sequential revision, because a roughly-right draft is close to a correct one and needs polishing rather than replacing. Harder problems favour more parallel exploration, because the first approach is likely to be the wrong approach entirely, and no amount of revising a wrong plan produces a right one.² The optimal mix at the very hardest end is genuinely unresolved — treat anyone who states it confidently with suspicion.

"Difficulty" here is operationalised the only way that is actually available to you: measure pass@1 on a sample of your own tasks and bin by it. You do not need a theory of difficulty; you need a histogram. This is the cheapest useful measurement in the whole course, and Lesson 12 builds on it.

Check yourself

Your agent writes an analysis that is structurally right but keeps making small errors in the final numbers. Where should the extra inference budget go?

Structurally right, locally wrong is the signature of an easy-to-medium problem in the difficulty binning — the draft is near the answer. Revision (ideally with a tool that can actually check the arithmetic, see Lesson 7) is the efficient spend. Parallel sampling would buy you fifty analyses with fifty different small errors, and no way to tell them apart.

The honest limit: where a bigger model still wins

Test-time compute is not a free substitute for capability. Snell et al. compared spending tokens at inference against spending them on pre-training and found the answer flips with difficulty: for easy and medium problems, additional inference compute beats a bigger model; for the hardest problems, the larger, more heavily pre-trained model still wins even at generous inference budgets.² Mirhoseini reports the same pattern from her lab: small open models keep getting more useful as you apply more test-time compute, but the genuinely hard tail still belongs to the frontier.

There is an asymmetry worth naming, though. Pre-training is paid once by someone with a datacentre; inference is paid every time, by you. Most people cannot choose to pre-train. Almost everyone can choose to sample five times — which is why this axis is the practical one even where it is not the theoretically superior one.

The catch, which is the next two lessons

Everything above assumes you can identify the good sample. Coverage is what your model could achieve with a perfect verifier. What you actually ship is coverage minus your selection error, and that subtraction is usually brutal. Lesson 3 measures it.

Retrieval practice

From memory: why does coverage across a benchmark follow a power law when the per-problem maths is exponential — and what does that fact tell you about where your sampling budget is going?

Per problem, pass@k = 1−(1−p)k, which saturates fast. A power law over the whole benchmark only emerges if p is distributed with a long tail toward zero — many easy items, and a heavy tail of very hard ones. Practically: the first sample resolves the easy mass, and every sample after that is being spent almost entirely on the hard tail. That is an argument for routing — detect the hard cases and spend there — rather than raising k uniformly.

What you can now do

You can state the difference between parallel and sequential test-time compute, pick between them from the shape of your failures, and give a number for the headroom sampling would buy on your own workload. You also know the limit: this dial does not manufacture capability the model lacks, and on the hardest tail a better model still wins.
