Lesson 11 of 14 · Self-Improving AI Agents

Deep research agents

Retrieval that happens when the reasoning needs it, and a note-taking step between the document and the prompt. Two changes that separate a research agent from a RAG pipeline.

≈10 minutes · Lecture 7 · The architecture closest to analysis work

A reasoning model with a knowledge gap does something specific and observable: it hedges. Uncertainty surfaces as vocabulary — perhaps, alternatively, likely — scattered through the reasoning chain. And because reasoning is sequential, a guess made early propagates: every subsequent step inherits it, and the final answer carries confidence it has not earned.

That is the problem Search-o1¹ addresses, and its diagnosis is the useful part: the hedging is a signal, and it tells you when to go and look something up.

Three architectures, in order of goodness

Retrieval timingWhat enters the promptFails when
Classic RAGOnce, up frontTop-k documentsLater steps need information you couldn't have known to fetch
Agentic RAGWhenever the model emits a searchRetrieved documents, wholeDocuments are long and noisy; the reasoning drowns in them
Search-o1Whenever the model emits a searchAn extracted, relevant summary of each documentThe extraction step drops something that mattered

The step from row one to row two is the one most people have already made: let the model decide when to retrieve, mid-reasoning, as many times as it needs. Single up-front retrieval is fine for "what was the weather" and hopeless for anything where step three's question depends on step two's answer.

The step from row two to row three is the one most people have not made, and it is where the gains are.

Reason-in-documents: take notes, don't paste

Why this matters more than it sounds: long-context reasoning degrades. Dropping ten documents into a reasoning chain assumes the model reasons as well over 40,000 tokens of mixed-relevance text as over a focused prompt, and it does not. Extraction is a context-quality intervention, and Lesson 13 is about why that dominates almost everything else in long runs.

A student asked the sharp question — isn't this just prompting the model to summarise before continuing? Largely yes, and the lecturer's answer is worth keeping: it is a workaround for a limitation that ought to disappear as long-context reasoning improves. That is honest, and it does not make it less useful today. Several such patterns in this course are scaffolding around a current weakness. Knowing which weakness a pattern serves tells you when to retire it.

The reported effect is measurable in an unusual way: the frequency of uncertainty markers in the reasoning chains drops substantially. The model stops hedging because it stopped guessing.

Check yourself

Your research agent retrieves well but produces vague, hedged conclusions. What is the most likely cause?

"Retrieves well but concludes vaguely" is the signature of good search feeding a drowning context. All four are real failure modes, and the way to tell them apart is to read the trace: if the right document was fetched and the right sentence is sitting in the context unused, the problem is dilution, and an extraction step fixes it. This is also why logging retrieved content separately from reasoning (Lesson 7) pays for itself.

Knowing what you don't know

The lecture flags the genuinely hard part, which the paper handles only partially: how does the model know what to look up? Detecting your own knowledge gap is exactly the thing models are bad at — they are systematically overconfident. Aggregate the log-probabilities of an answer and you will find a model 80% confident on questions it gets right half the time. Uncertainty markers in a reasoning chain are a usable proxy precisely because the calibrated signal isn't available.

The practical answer offered is not clever, and is better for it: decide in advance which parts of a question you will not trust the weights for. Identify the key entities, dates, figures and definitions, and fetch those on principle rather than waiting for the model to feel unsure. In your domain you already know which facts must come from the warehouse and which the model may supply from memory. Encode that; do not delegate it to the model's introspection.

Building one for analysis work

  • Retrieve mid-reasoning, repeatedly. One up-front fetch caps you at questions you could fully specify before starting — which excludes most interesting ones.
  • Put an extraction step inside every retrieval tool. Return notes, not payloads. This applies to your query results and file reads as much as to web search: a tool that returns 5,000 rows should return a characterisation of them.
  • Pin the facts that must be fetched. A list of entity types the agent must always look up beats hoping it notices its own ignorance.
  • Treat hedging as a metric. Counting uncertainty markers per run is a crude but genuinely useful proxy for grounding, and it costs a regular expression. Watch it move when you change retrieval.
  • Distrust stated confidence. Models are overconfident and will defend wrong answers. Confidence expressed in prose is not evidence.

The lecture also points at Search-R1, which learns the search behaviour with reinforcement learning rather than eliciting it by prompting. Same architecture, different acquisition route — and a clean illustration of the Lesson 9 rule: prompt-elicited behaviour first, trained behaviour only once the pattern is stable and the cost justifies it.

Retrieval practice

For one analysis task you'd delegate to an agent: which facts must never come from the model's weights, and what would the extraction step return instead of the raw tool output?

Worked example, a competitor pricing analysis. Must be fetched: current prices, product names and SKUs, dates of changes, anything with a number in it, and the definitions of internal metrics — all of which the model will happily invent plausibly. May come from weights: what a price elasticity is, how to structure the comparison, standard caveats about sample size. Extraction step: instead of returning a 200-row result set, return "42 SKUs matched; price range £4.99–£89.99; 6 changed in the last 30 days, listed below; 3 rows have null category" — a characterisation the reasoning can actually use, with the raw data still available on request. That last clause matters: extraction should narrow what enters context, not destroy access.

What you can now do

You can distinguish classic RAG, agentic RAG and reason-in-documents, and say what each fails at. You know that hedging vocabulary is a usable uncertainty proxy where calibrated confidence isn't, that models are overconfident by default, and that the fix for a drowning context is a note-taking step rather than a bigger window.
