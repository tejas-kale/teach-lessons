Lesson 14 of 14 · Self-Improving AI Agents

The frontier

Three bottlenecks the field knows it has, what "non-verifiable" actually means, and a way of reading the next paper that outlasts any particular result.

≈9 minutes · Lecture 9 · The lesson designed to expire slowly

The final lecture organises the open problems around the self-improvement loop you have been building since Lesson 1: generate candidates, verify them, train or select on what survives. Each of the three bottlenecks is a place that loop stalls — and each has a version that stalls your workflows, at a much smaller scale.

Bottleneck 1 — diversity collapse

Run the loop with one model and it stops paying after a handful of iterations. The diagnosis is lack of diversity: pre-training data is diverse because thousands of people wrote it over decades, whereas a single model sampling its own outputs produces variations on one voice — and raising the temperature does not fix this. It perturbs wording, not approach.

The proposed remedy is multiple specialised agents¹: several generators fine-tuned differently plus critic agents, exchanging and summarising each other's answers over rounds of debate. Measured by embedding dissimilarity, responses stay diverse across iterations where single-agent fine-tuning collapses — and accuracy keeps climbing.

The version you can run

The lecture calls it "the poor man's version" and it is most of the benefit: use different models. Different families have genuinely different failure modes, so their errors are closer to independent — which is the same property Lesson 5 needed for verifier ensembles and Lesson 10 needed for candidate clustering. Diversity of mechanism keeps turning out to be the scarce resource.

Bottleneck 2 — verification, again

Verification has been the binding constraint since Lesson 3, and the frontier version is sharper. In theorem proving, models produce invalid proofs and then, asked to check them, confidently declare them valid. LLM-as-judge fails outright, because the judge shares the generator's blind spots.

DeepSeek-Math-V2's² answer is to add a layer: a meta-verifier that reviews the verifier's critique. Do the issues it identified actually exist? Does the score follow from those issues? This catches the failure that makes weak judges dangerous — fabricated criticism, a judge inventing plausible-sounding faults. Humans annotate the meta-verification layer initially; once it learns, the labelling automates. Verifier and generator then improve each other in a loop.

The practitioner version is cheaper than it sounds. When a judge rejects an agent's output, ask a second pass: is this criticism specific, and is it true? False rejections are as costly as false acceptances and much harder to notice — an agent thrashing against an incorrect critique looks exactly like an agent failing at a hard task.

Bottleneck 3 — where do the problems come from?

The least-discussed constraint: training data for the loop needs prompts, and those are human-curated. Improving at IMO problems needs IMO experts to write them. As models approach and pass expert level, that supply becomes the ceiling.

The proposal is a model that generates its own tasks alongside solving them³, with the proposer rewarded for producing problems of the right difficulty — zero reward if the solver never succeeds, and none for trivial ones either. It targets the band where the solver sometimes succeeds and sometimes fails. Proposed tasks are validated by execution before entering training; a buffer of past tasks is sampled from to sustain diversity. Results reached state of the art on coding benchmarks with no human-curated prompts, and transferred to maths.

You have met that difficulty rule twice already: DAPO's dynamic sampling in Lesson 9, and the eval-curation advice that a benchmark you pass 100% of teaches you nothing. The principle is the same at every scale — learning happens where outcomes are mixed.

Check yourself

Which principle recurs across all three of this lecture's bottlenecks?

Diversity collapse is variation lost among generators; meta-verification restores meaningful variation in critiques (a judge that approves everything, or fabricates faults, carries no signal); task proposal manufactures variation in difficulty. A homogeneous system — whatever the dimension — stops learning. A and C are true statements from the lecture; D is the theme of the whole course but does not explain the third bottleneck.

What "non-verifiable" actually means

Asked which domains resist this machinery, the lecturers separate two cases that are worth keeping apart, because they call for different responses:

ExamplesWhy it blocks the loopWhat to do
Slow verificationScientific discovery, chip design simulation, wet-lab chemistryGround truth exists but takes days; RL needs thousands of iterationsBuild cheap proxies and validate them against the slow signal periodically
Genuinely subjectiveCreative writing, taste, strategyNo reward function exists; any modelled one gets hackedKeep a human in the loop; use rubrics for the decomposable parts only

Most of your work is the first kind, not the second. "We can't evaluate this" usually means "evaluating it takes a week and involves three people" — which is a proxy-design problem, not an impossibility. That distinction is worth pressing on whenever someone claims their domain is unmeasurable.

The open bet named at the end is worth watching: push self-improvement hard in verifiable domains and see how far the resulting capability transfers to domains you cannot verify. SWiRL's cross-tool generalisation (Lesson 9) and the task-proposal work's coding-to-maths transfer are both early evidence that it does.

How to read the next paper

The specific results above will age. This won't — for any new agent technique, ask:

  • Which axis is it on? Train-time, test-time, or the loop between them (Lesson 1). This tells you immediately whether you can adopt it or must wait to buy it.
  • What is the verifier, and how good is it? Every method has one, even when unnamed. If the paper is vague here, that is where its results are weakest.
  • Is the reported metric coverage or delivery? pass@k, majority@k and pass@1 are different claims (Lesson 3), and the gap between them is where enthusiasm goes to die.
  • What does it assume about the environment? Cheap undo? Fast feedback? Known ground truth? These assumptions decide whether it applies to your work far more than the headline number does.
  • What is the cost frontier? Accuracy at unstated cost is not a result. If cost is missing, assume it is bad.
  • Which bottleneck does it address? Diversity, verification, task supply — or none of the three, in which case ask what it is really for.
Retrieval practice — the closing exercise

Return to what you wrote in Lesson 1: which axis your workflow was on, and which of the five ingredients it lacked. Answer again now, and then name the single change you will actually make this month.

If the arc worked, your answer has moved from "it's a prompt and some tools" to something like: it's test-time only, single-sample, no verifier; coverage is probably well above pass@1 and I have never measured it. And the change is one of three: run the two-number test (Lesson 3), write the standing-context document (Lesson 13), or build the three-verifier ensemble (Lesson 5). All are days of work, not weeks. The two-number test should come first, because the other two are answers and it tells you which question you have.

Where this leaves you

You have the map (three axes), the constraint (verification, not generation), the levers (tuning dials), the measurement discipline (eval playbook), and a reading frame for whatever comes next. Which is the whole of what a practitioner needs from a research course.

The part you cannot get from lessons is wisdom — what actually happens when you run this on your data, with your constraints, in front of your colleagues. That comes from doing it and from arguing about it with people who also do it. RESOURCES.md lists communities worth joining; the evals discussion in particular gets sharper when there are other people's numbers in the room.

The course ends; the teaching doesn't. The most useful next session is not another lesson — it's running the Lesson 3 diagnostic on a real workflow and working out together what the numbers mean. Bring results and we'll decide what to build.
