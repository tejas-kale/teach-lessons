Buy or tune

This is the course's most technical territory and the least of it applies to you directly. So take it in the order that matters: the loop, the honest result, the failure conditions, and only then the decision.

The loop, in one sentence

If you keep one thing from this lesson

Train-time scaling is: take the model's own outputs, filter them cleverly, and fine-tune on what survives. Test-time compute is what produces the candidates; verification is what does the filtering; the fine-tune is what turns an expensive procedure into a cheap one. That is the entire self-improvement loop the course is named after.

It has a hard precondition. It only runs where verification is possible. The o-series data shown in Lecture 6 makes this vivid: win rates over a non-thinking model exceed 50% in programming, data analysis and mathematical calculation — domains where you can close the loop — and are markedly weaker in personal writing and editing, where you cannot. If your domain has no verifier, this axis is largely closed to you, which is why Lessons 4 to 7 came first.

STaR: bootstrapping reasoning from almost nothing

STaR¹ is the simplest complete instance of the loop, and worth understanding because it exposes every one of its assumptions. Start with a handful of worked examples. Few-shot prompt the model over a training set of problems whose answers you know. Keep the generated reasoning chains that reached the right answer. Fine-tune on those. Repeat.

The wrinkle is what to do about the failures, which carry no signal. STaR's answer — rationalisation — is to hand the model the correct answer and ask it to explain how to get there, then train on the explanation with the hint removed. This pulls harder problems into the training set instead of discarding them.

The students immediately found the flaw, and it is a good one: given the answer, a model can produce a fluent rationale that does not actually derive it. You then fine-tune on confident nonsense. STaR itself applies no filter at this step; later work adds a process reward model there. It is the Lesson 4 false-positive problem, arriving exactly where it does most damage.

Three limits, all of which generalise well beyond STaR:

  • It cannot exceed the base model's reach. If a problem class is entirely outside what the model can ever stumble into, no amount of bootstrapping finds it. You amplify existing capability; you do not create it.
  • It plateaus. Iterations stop paying after a few rounds.
  • Final-answer correctness is a proxy for reasoning quality, and an imperfect one. You keep some invalid reasoning that reached the right answer, and discard some valid reasoning that didn't.

The honest result about RL

DeepSeek-Math² is famous for GRPO — dropping PPO's learned critic and computing the advantage from a group of sampled answers instead: reward minus the group mean, divided by the group's spread. Fewer model copies in memory, so RL scales further. It took a 7B model past 50% on MATH, which at the time was a size class ahead of expectations.

But the result you should carry is a different one. Their RL improved majority@k and did not improve pass@k.

That should recalibrate what you expect a fine-tune to buy. It is a reliability instrument, not a capability instrument — it makes the model produce its good answer more often. Which is genuinely valuable, and is also the same thing a good verifier buys you, at a small fraction of the effort. That comparison is the heart of the decision below.

Check yourself

Your agent solves a task correctly in 3 of 10 runs. What does the DeepSeek-Math result suggest about fine-tuning it?

Three in ten means the capability exists and is unreliably surfaced — exactly what RL improves, and exactly what selection improves. Given a fine-tune costs weeks and a verifier costs an afternoon, try the verifier first and reserve the fine-tune for when you have a stable task, a real eval, and a per-call cost worth optimising. B inverts the finding: RL moved consistency, not coverage.

Why RL runs break, and the law underneath

Naively scaling GRPO to a larger model fails in specific ways, which DAPO³ set out to fix: entropy collapses (the model gets over-confident and stops exploring), training destabilises, and response length grows without bound. Two of its fixes are worth knowing:

  • Asymmetric clipping. PPO's symmetric clip treats increases and decreases the same, which caps how much a low-probability token can be promoted and quietly kills exploration. Allowing larger upward moves keeps entropy alive.
  • Dynamic sampling. Oversample, then discard groups where every sample was correct or every sample was wrong. Those groups produce zero advantage after normalisation and contribute nothing to the gradient. Keeping only the mixed groups preserves an effective batch size.

Dynamic sampling is a special case of the law that runs through the whole lecture: you need a distribution of rewards. All-correct teaches nothing. All-wrong teaches nothing. Learning happens on the band in between.

This applies directly to your eval sets, which is where you will actually use it. A benchmark your agent passes 100% of, or fails 100% of, contains no information about your next change. The tasks worth keeping are the ones that sometimes work. When curating an eval set, deliberately over-weight that band — and keep a few from each end only as guardrails against regression.

Two more findings that transfer

Data curation beat the algorithm. DeepSeek-Math's largest gains came before any RL: curating maths content from web crawl rather than relying on arXiv, and starting from a code-pretrained model, which improved both reasoning and tool use. The unglamorous input work outperformed the clever optimiser. This is the most reliable pattern in applied machine learning and it has not stopped being true.

Filter for process, not outcome — if you are doing RL. SWiRL⁴ found that training on process-filtered trajectories (steps judged sound) beat outcome-filtered ones for RL, because outcome filtering keeps only problems the model already solves, teaching it nothing new. For supervised fine-tuning the preference reverses — SFT is imitation, so incorrect final answers actively hurt. SWiRL also generalised across tools and domains: trained on maths with a calculator, it improved at multi-hop QA with a search tool. The model learned how to work in steps and invoke a tool, not one tool.

The decision

SymptomDo thisNot this

Wrong output format or house stylePrompt, then few-shot examplesFine-tune
Missing domain knowledgeRetrieval, better contextFine-tune
Capability genuinely absentStronger model, better tools, decompositionFine-tune — it amplifies, it doesn't create
All of the above solved; per-call cost now dominatesNow consider distilling your working pipeline into a smaller model—

The honest summary: fine-tuning is right when you have a stable task, a trustworthy eval, thousands of verified examples, and a cost or latency problem that a smaller model would solve. In practice the last item is the real trigger — you fine-tune to make something cheap that already works, not to make something work. Note too that at the frontier labs, RL remains a small fraction of total training compute, growing but nowhere near parity. If it were the easy win, it would not be.

Retrieval practice

Explain to a colleague who wants to fine-tune on your agent's successful runs why that is probably premature, and what the two cheaper experiments are.

Three points. (1) Training on outcome-filtered successes keeps runs that were right for the wrong reasons and teaches the reasoning that produced them. (2) The DeepSeek-Math result suggests the gain would be consistency, not new capability — and a verifier buys the same consistency in an afternoon rather than a month. (3) Successes are, by definition, the problems the model already solves, so they contain the least information. The two cheaper experiments: run the two-number test from Lesson 3 to see whether this is a selection or a capability problem, and build a three-verifier ensemble (Lesson 5) to close whatever gap it reveals. If both are done and per-call cost is still the binding constraint, fine-tuning becomes a reasonable conversation.

What you can now do

You can hold the self-improvement loop in one sentence, state what RL actually buys (consistency, not coverage), name the conditions under which training runs collapse, and — most usefully — apply the reward-distribution law to the eval sets you curate. You also have a defensible answer to "should we fine-tune this?", which is usually no, for stateable reasons.
