Lesson 1 of 14 · Self-Improving AI Agents

Where the returns moved

The single argument the whole course hangs off: making the model bigger stopped being the best way to spend a dollar, and three other places to spend it took over.

≈9 minutes · Gives you the map every later lesson is drawn on

You use agents every day. You have probably noticed that your leverage over them is uneven: sometimes a better prompt fixes everything, sometimes nothing you write helps, and occasionally a model upgrade silently solves a problem you had been engineering around for a month. That unevenness is not random. It reflects which axis of improvement your problem actually sits on — and there are now three axes, not one.

The old axis, and why it flattened

From roughly 2020 to 2024 the field had one dial. Kaplan et al.'s scaling laws¹ showed test loss falling predictably as you increased compute, dataset size, and parameter count — three log-log lines, all sloping down. BERT at 340M → GPT-2 at 1.5B → GPT-3 at 175B → PaLM at 540B. Each step bought measurable capability, and two capabilities appeared that nobody had ordered: robust few-shot learning, and chain-of-thought reasoning that only showed up above a size threshold.²

"Emergence" is contested — some of it is an artefact of discontinuous metrics like exact-match accuracy. The contest doesn't matter for your purposes. What matters is that the era's answer to every capability question was wait for the next model, and that answer has expired.

CS329A opens by stating the thing plainly: that curve began to saturate around 2024.³ Not stopped — saturated. Returns per unit of pre-training compute got small enough that the frontier labs went looking elsewhere. Where they went is the entire subject of this course, and of your job.

The three axes that replaced it

Axis 1 — train-time scaling

Spend compute before deployment, but on reinforcement learning against checkable rewards rather than on more next-token prediction. Math with known answers, code with passing tests. This is where the reasoning models came from. As a practitioner you mostly consume this axis: it arrives as a model release. Lesson 8 covers the small slice you can act on.

Axis 2 — test-time scaling

Spend compute during the request: think longer, sample repeatedly, search over candidates, verify before returning. This axis is almost entirely under your control — it is a property of the harness you write, not of the weights. Lessons 2 through 7 live here, and this is where most of your available leverage is.

Axis 3 — self-improvement

Close the loop between the two. In its industrial form this means generating synthetic training data with an expensive test-time procedure and fine-tuning on the verified wins. In the form you will actually use, it means an agent that accumulates verified experience — notes, skills, corrected failure cases — and gets better without anyone touching the weights. Lesson 9.

The sentence to keep

Every technique in this course is an answer to one question: how do you convert compute into reliability? Pre-training answered "spend it on parameters". The current answer is "spend it on generating candidates, and spend the rest on checking them".

Check yourself

A vendor claims their agent "reasons for longer on hard problems and gets better results". Which axis is that claim about?

The compute is spent at inference, so it is axis 2. The distractors are all true statements that are answers to a different question — which is exactly how these claims blur in marketing copy. Sorting a claim onto the right axis tells you who controls the dial: axis 2 you can tune yourself, axis 1 you can only buy.

Where verification enters, and why it never leaves

Axes 2 and 3 both quietly assume something: that you can tell a good output from a bad one. Sample a hundred solutions and one is right — you have gained nothing unless you can find it. Generate synthetic training data — it poisons the model unless you can filter it.

The course names this directly in the first lecture, in an aside that turns out to be the whole plot: models can generate an enormous amount of plausible-looking material cheaply, and whether it is any good requires a feedback loop. Where the feedback is crisp — math, code, anything rule-based — improvement compounds. Where it isn't — creative work, strategy, most analysis — human judgement becomes the bottleneck, and robust verification stays hard.³

The gap between "can produce" and "can recognise" has a name — the generation–verification gap — and Lesson 4 is entirely about it. Notice that it reframes your problem usefully: "the model can't do this" is a wall, but "my verifier is too weak" is a to-do item.

This is the most important thing to carry out of Lesson 1, because it changes what you build. Most people, facing a disappointing agent, work on the generator: better prompt, better model, more context. Often the generator was already producing a correct answer somewhere in its distribution, and the system threw it away.

What an agent actually is, structurally

The course uses a plain decomposition, matching Anthropic's workflow taxonomy⁵. An agentic system is some arrangement of five ingredients:

IngredientWhat it doesCovered in
LLM callsGenerate candidate text or actionsLesson 2
Tool callsTouch the world — search, code execution, APIs, databasesLesson 6
VerifiersCheck an output against ground truth: tests, types, schemas, executionsLessons 4, 6
Judges / criticsScore an output where ground truth doesn't existLesson 5
OrchestrationChaining, routing, parallelisation, planner–worker, evaluator–optimiserLessons 7, 10

Two things follow. First, when a run fails you can localise the failure to an ingredient rather than blaming "the model" — that diagnostic habit is what Lesson 12 formalises. Second, the ingredients you are most likely to be under-using are the middle two. Most working agents are LLM calls plus tools plus hope.

Retrieval practice

Pick one agent workflow you actually run. Without scrolling up: which of the three axes is it currently exploiting, and which of the five ingredients does it lack? Two or three sentences.

The common shape: it is a single LLM call plus a few tools (axis 2, but only barely — one sample, no search), it has no verifier and no judge, and its "orchestration" is a prompt asking the model to be careful. That configuration has one improvement lever, the prompt, which is why prompt-tuning feels like the whole job. Adding a verifier gives you a second lever and, more importantly, a measurement. Keep whatever you wrote — Lesson 13 asks you to re-read it.

What you can now do

You can sort any claim, paper, or product pitch onto one of three axes, and you know which of them you control. You know that verification, not generation, is the usual binding constraint. And you have a five-part vocabulary for describing what your own agents are made of — which is what makes the rest of this course actionable rather than interesting.
