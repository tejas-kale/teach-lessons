Lesson 3 of 14 · Self-Improving AI Agents

Coverage is not accuracy

The single most useful idea in the course: your agent probably already produced the right answer and then discarded it. The distance between those two facts has a name.

≈9 minutes · Lecture 2 · The idea the next four lessons all serve

Lesson 2 ended on an assumption we quietly smuggled in: that after drawing k samples you can pick the good one. Drop that assumption and the whole picture changes.

Coverage — pass@k — is what your system could deliver with a perfect oracle sitting at the end. What it actually delivers is coverage minus whatever your selection mechanism gets wrong. That subtraction is the generation–verification gap, and for most real workloads it is the dominant term.³

Why the obvious selectors plateau

Majority voting (self-consistency⁴) takes the most common answer. It works beautifully when the model is usually right and occasionally slips — the errors are idiosyncratic, the correct answer is modal. It fails exactly where you need help. On the hardest problems, Lecture 2 reports correct answers appearing once or twice in ten thousand samples. A rare correct answer is, by construction, invisible to a vote. Majority voting is a mechanism for suppressing noise, and on hard problems the correct answer is the outlier.

This also explains a failure you have probably seen: an agent that becomes more confidently wrong when you ask it to check its work by trying several times. You have built a machine for reinforcing its modal error.

Reward-model ranking (best-of-n with a learned scorer) does better — it can in principle recognise a rare correct answer without needing it to be popular. But learned reward models inherit the same blind spots as the generator, degrade off-distribution, and are trained on preferences that are only a proxy for correctness. In the MATH results the gap to true coverage stays large.

The general lesson: selectors that only look at the text are weak. Both failing methods judge answers by comparing them to each other or to a learned prior. Neither one contacts reality.

Check yourself

You raise k from 10 to 200 with majority-vote selection and accuracy barely moves. What has most likely happened?

You cannot distinguish these from the accuracy number alone — which is the point. Measure coverage separately, with an oracle or with careful manual grading on a small slice. If coverage rose while accuracy stayed flat, the bottleneck is selection, and buying a better model would be spending money on the wrong thing. If coverage also stayed flat, then B or A is live. Two numbers, entirely different diagnoses.

Where verification is easy, and where it is not

The gap closes when you have something that contacts ground truth. Lecture 2 lays out the domains where that is cheap, and they have a common structure: checking is a fundamentally easier problem than producing.

DomainVerifierWhy it's easy
Formal mathsProof checker (Lean, Coq)Validity is mechanically decidable
CodeUnit tests, type checker, compilerWriting a test is far cheaper than writing the program
Kernel / compiler generationDifferential testing — run both, compare outputsA reference implementation already exists
Language / format translationRound-trip or equivalence checksSame as above: the source is the oracle
Data pipelines (yours)Schema checks, row counts, invariants, reconciliation against a known totalYou already write these; you just don't feed them back to the agent
Analysis, writing, strategy—No oracle. This is where the gap lives, and Lessons 5 and 6 are about it.

The row that should interest you most is the fifth. Data work is unusually rich in cheap partial verifiers that practitioners already trust and already write — and then use only as a post-hoc alarm for humans, rather than as a signal the agent can iterate against. Turning an existing assertion into an agent-visible check is the highest ratio of leverage to effort available in this entire course.

Verifiers are not free of error either

Lecture 2 is candid about this. Unit tests with poor coverage pass bad code. Automated grading of MATH answers was manually spot-checked at roughly 97–98% agreement — good, not perfect. A weak verifier does not merely fail to help; under heavy sampling it becomes a target to be gamed, since drawing a thousand samples is an efficient search for whatever your check does not examine. Lesson 5 makes this precise.

The reframe worth stealing

When an agent underperforms, the instinctive move is to work on the generator: better prompt, better model, more context. The gap says: measure first, because there are two different diseases with identical symptoms.

  • Low coverage. Even the best of k attempts is wrong. This is a genuine capability problem. Better model, better tools, better decomposition (Lesson 8).
  • High coverage, low accuracy. The answer is in there and you are picking the wrong one. Nothing about the generator needs to change. Build a verifier (Lessons 4–7).

The second case is enormously more common than practitioners assume, and it is also the cheaper one to fix. "The model can't do this" is a wall. "My selector is weak" is a ticket.

Retrieval practice

Design the smallest experiment that would tell you which of the two diseases one of your own agent workflows has. Be concrete: what do you run, what do you record, what does each outcome imply?

Take 20–30 real tasks with known-good outcomes. Run each 10 times. Record two numbers: how often run 1 succeeded (≈ pass@1, what you ship today) and how often any of the 10 succeeded (coverage). If coverage is much higher, you have a selection problem and your next build is a verifier. If coverage is barely above pass@1, extra sampling is wasted and you need capability — a stronger model, better tools, or a different task decomposition. Total cost: a few hundred calls and an afternoon of grading. Almost nobody does this, which is why almost everybody guesses.

What you can now do

You can name the generation–verification gap, explain why majority voting and reward-model ranking both plateau, and — most importantly — you have a two-number diagnostic that tells you whether to invest in the generator or the selector. That diagnostic is the backbone of the evaluation work in Lessons 12 and 13.
