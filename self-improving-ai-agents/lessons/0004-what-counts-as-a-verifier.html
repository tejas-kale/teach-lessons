What counts as a verifier

Grading the answer versus grading the working. Four years of research on that distinction, reduced to the handful of results that should change what you build.

≈10 minutes · Lecture 3 · Unlocks the ORM/PRM vocabulary everyone uses and few define

Lesson 3 left you with a job: build a selector that contacts reality. The research literature has been working on that job since 2021, and it converged on a distinction worth internalising — where in the trajectory you attach the score.

The founding result, and its ratio

OpenAI's 2021 GSM8K work introduced both the benchmark and the recipe.¹ Fine-tune a generator; sample 100 solutions per problem; label each by whether its final answer matched the known one; train a model to predict that label; at test time, sample and take the highest-scoring candidate. The headline: with enough verifier training data, this beats simply fine-tuning the generator harder on the same data. Below roughly a thousand labelled problems it does not — the verifier hasn't learned enough to be worth consulting.

Note what the verifier route preserves: your base model stays general. Fine-tuning specialises the generator onto one distribution. A verifier leaves the generator alone and adds a filter you can swap, retrain, or remove. For anyone maintaining several workflows on one model, that difference matters more than the accuracy delta.

One ablation from that paper deserves to be a rule of thumb. They compared two allocations of a fixed budget: large generator + small verifier, versus small generator + large verifier. The first won. The intuition is the one you already have — generating is harder than checking — but it inverts a common instinct, which is to make the judge the most expensive model in the pipeline. If you must choose, spend on the generator and keep the checker cheap.

The result nobody expects

Verifier-selected accuracy on GSM8K rose with sample count up to roughly 400 and then declined. Not plateaued — declined. With enough candidates, some wrong-but-plausible answer eventually outscores the right one; you are running an efficient search for the verifier's blind spots. Every verifier has a k beyond which it starts working against you, and you find it by measuring, not by reasoning.

Why grading the working is better

Let's Verify Step by Step² made the ORM/PRM comparison directly, with a dataset (PRM800K) of 800,000 human step-level labels. Four findings that carry over to anything you build:

  • PRMs beat ORMs, and beat majority voting. Notably, a PRM can identify correct solutions that appear in under 5% of samples — precisely the rare-correct case where voting is structurally blind.
  • PRMs kill false positives. Models reach right answers through wrong reasoning more often than is comfortable. Outcome supervision rewards that, and if you use it to generate training data you are actively selecting for it. Process supervision does not.
  • PRMs are more label-efficient per unit of annotation, despite needing more labels per solution — each step is a separate training signal.
  • PRMs generalise further under distribution shift. Interestingly, plain majority voting generalises better than an ORM — the ORM overfits its training distribution. The PRM beats both.

There is a cost: a PRM introduces a threshold you now have to tune, and it can penalise an unconventional-but-correct path. Current practice is to combine both signals rather than choose.

You plan to fine-tune on your agent's successful runs. Which verifier type matters most, and why?

This is the false-positive problem with teeth in it. Outcome-filtered data contains lucky runs — right answer, incoherent path — and fine-tuning on them teaches the incoherent path. The distortion compounds over rounds of self-improvement, which is exactly the loop Lesson 10 warns about. Getting step labels without humans

PRM800K required an enormous annotation effort, which puts PRMs out of reach for most teams. Math-Shepherd³ removed that constraint with a trick you can implement in an afternoon: define the quality of a step as its potential to reach a correct final answer.

Two failure modes are worth stating, because the lecture's students found them immediately. On genuinely hard problems no rollout succeeds, so every step scores zero and you get no signal at all — the method is blind exactly where you need it. And a wrong step that happens to be recoverable gets labelled correct, because the rollouts route around it. More rollouts mitigate both, at linear cost.

Notice the shape here, because it recurs for the rest of the course: spend inference compute to manufacture supervision you don't have. That is the self-improvement loop from Lesson 1 in its most concrete form, and it requires exactly one thing — an outcome you can check.

Translating this to work you actually do

You are unlikely to train a PRM. The transferable ideas are structural:

  • Attach checks to steps, not just to the end. An agent doing a five-stage data pipeline should be checked after each stage — schema, row count, key uniqueness — not only on the final chart. This is a PRM made of assertions, it costs nothing, and it converts a silent 40-minute failure into a 30-second one.
  • Watch for right-answer-wrong-reasoning whenever you use agent output as training data, as examples, or as a template. Outcome checks alone will let it through.
  • Find your verifier's ceiling. Sweep k and plot selected accuracy. Somewhere it turns over. Ship below that point.
  • Keep the checker cheap and the generator strong — the size-ratio result, and a considerable cost saving.
  • Score steps by rollout when you have a checkable outcome and want to know which intermediate choice went wrong: re-run from step k a few times and see how often it recovers. This is a debugging technique, not just a training one.
Retrieval practice

From memory: name the two things a PRM catches that an ORM does not, and the two things that make PRMs expensive. Then say which single step of a workflow you own is currently unchecked.

Catches: (1) right answer via wrong reasoning — the false-positive case; (2) rare-correct solutions that appear in a tiny minority of samples, which voting cannot see. It also enables mid-generation pruning, which an ORM structurally cannot. Costs: (1) step-level labels, historically human, unless you use rollout labelling; (2) a new threshold hyperparameter, plus the risk of penalising unusual-but-valid paths. The last part is the one to act on this week.

What you can now do

You can use ORM and PRM correctly, explain why process supervision is worth its cost, generate step labels without annotators, and you know two counterintuitive facts — that verifier quality degrades past a critical k, and that the generator should be the bigger model.
