Lesson 13 of 14 · Self-Improving AI Agents

Long horizons and context

Why long tasks fail in five specific ways, and the finding that explains most of the gap between what agents can do and what they do for you: they arrive as contractors, not maintainers.

≈10 minutes · Lecture 8 · The lesson about your unfair advantage

Lesson 12 established the reliability gap — an hour of capability at 50% success, a quarter of that at 80%. This lesson is about what fills the gap. It is not one thing, and none of it is mysterious.

Five ways long runs fail

The failure analysis in Lecture 8 gives a taxonomy stable enough to classify against.¹

FailureWhat it looks like in a traceWhat helps
Poor planningSteps are each reasonable; the decomposition solves an adjacent problemMake the plan an explicit artefact you can inspect before execution (Lesson 8)
Poor tool choiceReaches for the wrong instrument, or an inadequate oneFewer, better-described tools; make capabilities and limits explicit
Reasoning / arithmetic errorsConfident mental calculation, quietly wrongA tool. Never let it do arithmetic in its head (Lesson 7)
Premature abandonmentStops and declares done, having not met the goalAn explicit, checkable completion criterion — it often doesn't know what done means
Repetitive loopsRepeats a failing action because it remains the highest-probability actionLoop detection; a forced strategy change; richer error messages so the next attempt differs

Reasoning models reduced the repetitive-loop failure substantially relative to earlier models, but the categories persist. Notice that four of the five have cheap structural mitigations that cost no model quality at all — which is the point of classifying rather than lamenting.

"It doesn't know when it is done" is worth dwelling on. A human professional carries an implicit standard of completion. An agent has whatever you wrote in the prompt, and "produce a good analysis" is not a completion criterion. Stating one — done means every figure traces to a query and the three named questions are answered — removes a whole failure class for the price of a sentence.

The finding that reframes everything: contractor versus maintainer

On internal codebases, people without prior context — contractors — take somewhere between 5 and 18 times longer than the maintainers who work there daily. And model performance tracks the contractor times, not the maintainer times.¹

The reframe

Your agent is not a weak expert. It is a capable professional with no context — dropped into your codebase, your warehouse, your conventions, on their first morning, every single time. Most of what you experience as "the model isn't good enough" is the same penalty you would impose on a talented new hire by telling them nothing.

GDPval found the matching result from the other direction: strip context out of the prompts and win rates fall, and — more tellingly — the models struggle to work out what to work on at all. As the lecture puts it, real work is context-heavy, and much of professional skill is knowing what to prioritise and where the bodies are buried. Benchmarks measure whether a smart person with no context can do the task. They do not measure what someone embedded in the work can do.

This is genuinely good news, because context is the input you control and no benchmark can. It is also the most under-exploited lever available to you — considerably more so than any prompt-engineering technique.

Four strategies for context

More context is not automatically better — Lesson 11 showed a reasoning chain drowning in retrieved documents. What matters is relevant context, which needs managing:

  • Write the standing context down. Schema quirks, naming conventions, which tables are stale, what a good output looks like, the three things reviewers always ask. This is the highest-value document you can write, it is reusable across every agent you run, and its absence is what makes your agent a contractor.
  • Compact rather than accumulate. Long runs collect transcript. Periodically replace history with a summary of state and decisions — the reason-in-documents move from Lesson 11, applied to the agent's own trace.
  • Fan out into fresh contexts. Independent sub-tasks (Lesson 8) each get a clean window and return a short result. This is context hygiene as much as parallelism, and it is why decomposition helps even when nothing runs concurrently.
  • Externalise state. A file the agent writes progress and decisions to survives context loss, gives you something to inspect mid-run, and lets a failed run resume rather than restart. Cheap, and it converts a catastrophic failure into a recoverable one.
Check yourself

An agent does well on short tasks and degrades badly past about thirty minutes. What is the first thing to try?

Degradation with length is the signature of accumulating context and cascading error, and the structural fix is cheap and available today. A larger window does not help much, because the problem is dilution rather than truncation — reasoning quality falls over long noisy contexts well before the window fills. A is the expensive move, and worth trying only after the structural one fails. Note that checkpointing also converts one long unreliable run into several short reliable ones, which is exactly what the METR curves say you want.

What is actually driving the improvement

The lecture's list of capabilities behind the lengthening horizon is a checklist for your own harness, because each has a harness-level counterpart:

Model capabilityYour equivalent lever
Better logical reasoningThinking budget; explicit planning step
Better tool useTool design and rich failure messages (Lesson 7)
Fewer repetitive behavioursLoop detection; forced strategy change on repeat failure
Error recoveryCheckpoints; a written reflection after each failure (Lesson 8)
Goal awareness and stateExternalised state file; explicit completion criteria

The class also raised re-planning: strong agents plan, execute, discover the plan was wrong, and plan again. If your harness treats the plan as fixed after step one, you have engineered out the recovery behaviour that makes long tasks work at all.

Retrieval practice

Write the first ten lines of the standing-context document for your domain — the things a brilliant new colleague would need on day one that they could not work out alone.

The shape that works: which tables are authoritative and which are deprecated-but-not-deleted; the column whose name lies about its contents; the timezone convention and where it is violated; which metric definition is the official one when three exist; the filter everyone forgets (test accounts, internal orders); what the weekly numbers are actually used for and by whom; the caveat every analysis must carry; the three questions your reviewer always asks; who to ask when something looks wrong; and what "done" means here. None of this is in the schema, all of it is in your head, and every one of your agents is currently working without it.

What you can now do

You can classify a long-run failure into one of five categories and name the structural mitigation for each. More importantly, you have the contractor/maintainer reframe: the gap between benchmark agents and useful agents is mostly context, and context is the input you own. The standing-context document is the most valuable thing you could write this month.

The standing-context document is worth drafting together. It is hard to write alone precisely because the knowledge is invisible to whoever has it — that is what makes it valuable. Being interrogated about your domain by someone who doesn't share your assumptions is the fastest way to extract it.
